# Own Your Shit: Accountability Without Shame

I have fucked up.

Spectacularly.

I've failed people. I've failed myself. I've said things I shouldn't have said, done things I shouldn't have done, and made decisions that, looking back, make me wonder what the fuck I was thinking.

And you know what?

**I'm okay with that.**

I'm human.

I make mistakes. Sometimes I recognize them immediately. Sometimes it takes me a year. Sometimes it takes me fifteen fucking years.

But eventually, when I recognize something I've done wrong, I own it.

And the older I get, the more I realize something that genuinely baffles me:

**Why is admitting you've fucked up so goddamn difficult for people?**

## Failure Is Not Shame

I once got so drunk at a bar that I went to the bathroom, sat on the toilet, and apparently fell asleep.

Some guy had to drag me out.

The next day, I went back to the same bar. The guy reminded me what happened.

I thanked him.

Seriously. Thank you for helping me.

Then he started giving me shit about how drunk I'd been.

And I laughed.

*"You want a cookie?"*

What exactly was he expecting?

Was I supposed to bow my head in humiliation? Apologize for existing? Spend the next week contemplating the terrible shame of having fallen asleep on a fucking toilet?

I got drunk. I fell asleep. He helped me. I thanked him.

End of story.

Now, getting that drunk wasn't exactly a brilliant decision. I can acknowledge that without needing to feel ashamed of myself.

**There's a difference between recognizing your mistakes and being ashamed of who you are.**

I can be an amazing human being to one person and a complete disappointment to another.

I can be brilliant at certain things and an absolute disaster at others.

Some people think I'm a mentor. Others think I'm a failure.

And?

I'm still here.

I'm still living.

None of these things requires me to pretend I'm somebody I'm not.

## Fifteen Years Later

I was married once.

Five years. A church wedding. Vows. *Till death do us part.*

I believed in those words.

I was young, around 27 when the problems began, and I thought I understood what being a husband meant.

Provide.

Work hard. Make money. Give your wife a comfortable life.

At one point, I worked three jobs. I made around $200,000 in a year.

And somehow, in my mind, I still wasn't doing enough.

So what did I do?

I worked harder.

What I didn't understand was that being a provider and being emotionally present were two entirely different responsibilities.

I knew how to do one.

I didn't know how to balance both.

My wife became unhappy. Eventually, she became involved with someone at work who gave her the attention I wasn't giving her.

And here's the thing.

**I didn't care about the affair nearly as much as I cared about losing her.**

I loved that woman.

I was willing to work through it. I wanted to become a better husband. I wanted to repair our marriage.

I started trying harder. Bringing her lunch at work. Being attentive. Making the efforts I should have understood how to make years earlier.

But she was already done.

And the harder I tried, the more frustrated she became.

I couldn't understand it.

I thought marriage was forever.

She no longer wanted the marriage.

Fifteen years later, I finally understand something I couldn't understand then.

**Sometimes, by the time someone begins trying, the other person has already spent years letting go.**

And no amount of effort can manufacture feelings that no longer exist.

I don't think about my ex-wife very often anymore. Perhaps once a year.

I hope she's happy. I hope she found peace.

But if I could speak to her today, there's something I would tell her.

*"I'm sorry. I didn't understand how to be both a provider and an emotionally present husband. I thought I was doing what I was supposed to do. I see now where I failed you. I'm sorry I couldn't do better."*

Not because I want her back.

Not because I expect forgiveness.

Not because I need anything from her.

Simply because I can look back at my own behavior and recognize my mistakes.

**That's accountability.**

And I don't need somebody to demand it from me.

## The Other Side of the Door

Recently, I've found myself on the other side of that experience.

A family member wants to reconnect.

He's trying. I can see that. I don't doubt that some of his efforts are sincere.

But after years of disappointment, distance, and conversations that never seemed to address what mattered, I reached a point where I no longer wanted the relationship.

I told him so.

Clearly.

I wasn't asking for a break. I wasn't asking for time.

I was saying goodbye.

And he responded by telling me he would give me space, that he wouldn't give up, and that perhaps time would bring us back together.

But here's what he doesn't understand.

**Time is what brought me here.**

I didn't wake up one morning and decide I no longer wanted my brother in my life.

It took years.

Years of hoping. Years of trying. Years of wanting something that never became what I needed it to be.

And now he wants to try.

I recognize the effort.

I remember being the person who tried desperately to save a relationship after the other person had already checked out.

I know how much that hurts.

And that makes me sad for him.

But I cannot give someone a relationship simply because I understand their pain.

I wish him peace. I wish him happiness.

I just won't be part of it.

## The Apology Nobody Asked For

Here's something I realized while thinking about all these relationships.

I've spent my life reflecting on my own behavior.

Sometimes I've reached out to people years after we'd parted ways to acknowledge something I'd done wrong.

I wasn't trying to resume the relationship.

I wasn't trying to get anything.

I simply recognized that I'd failed someone, and I wanted to acknowledge it.

Of course, the other person doesn't owe me a response. Even a sincere apology doesn't entitle me to their attention.

That's their choice.

But I've noticed something in my own relationships.

People have wanted to reconnect with me, move forward, forget the past, and start again.

And yet I've rarely experienced the kind of spontaneous, specific accountability I've tried to practice myself.

Not a generic *"I'm sorry things went wrong."*

Not *"We both made mistakes."*

Not *"Life is too short, let's move on."*

Something much simpler.

*"I wasn't there when you needed me."*

*"I dismissed what you were going through."*

*"I behaved badly."*

*"I failed you in this way."*

*"I'm sorry."*

That's it.

That's the fucking thing.

And I've realized that **someone acknowledging their mistakes would actually make me respect them more, not less.**

I'm not asking for perfection.

I'm not looking for flawless human beings.

Christ, look at me.

I'm the last person who should expect perfection from anybody.

I'm asking for integrity.

## I'm Not Your Teacher

But here's where it gets interesting.

I refuse to tell people what they need to apologize for.

I refuse to walk someone through every mistake they made, explain exactly how they hurt me, and then teach them the appropriate words to say.

Because what exactly have we accomplished at that point?

I've effectively written their apology for them.

I don't want that.

I want someone to examine their own behavior because they believe it's worth examining.

Not because I've demanded it.

Not because they want something from me.

Not because apologizing might reopen a door I've closed.

**Because they recognize their own responsibility.**

People occasionally regard me as a mentor or a teacher.

And I laugh.

Who the fuck gave me that job?

I'm another human being trying to figure things out.

I don't have some special authority to tell people how to live.

I don't want that authority.

And I certainly don't want the responsibility of managing someone else's conscience.

You can live however you want.

You can refuse to acknowledge your mistakes.

You can tell yourself whatever story makes you comfortable.

That's your business.

But whether I choose to have a meaningful relationship with you?

That's mine.

## Integrity Without Perfection

I think one of the strangest things about human beings is how desperately we sometimes defend ourselves against the possibility that we've made a mistake.

As though admitting failure somehow makes us less worthy.

As though being wrong about something means being wrong about everything.

I don't understand that.

I've done plenty of things I regret.

I've also done plenty of things I'm proud of.

Both are true.

Neither requires me to invent a different version of myself.

And I don't think accountability needs to come with humiliation, punishment, or shame.

Quite the opposite.

**The freedom to admit you've fucked up without hating yourself for it is a remarkable thing.**

You don't need to pretend.

You don't need to defend every decision you've ever made.

You don't need to rewrite history so that you're always the hero.

You can simply say:

*"Yeah. I fucked that up."*

And then try to do better.

Will you always succeed?

Of course not.

You'll probably fuck something else up next Tuesday.

Welcome to being human.

## Just Be

Tonight, while thinking about all this, one of my favorite songs came on.

Tiësto and Kirsty Hawkshaw's *Just Be*.

And something about that moment felt almost absurdly appropriate.

I've spent years trying to understand relationships, disappointment, forgiveness, responsibility, and what it means to be at peace with myself.

And I've realized that I don't particularly care whether people consider me successful or unsuccessful, good or bad, admirable or disappointing.

I'm all of those things, depending on who's looking.

I don't need to be the hero in anybody's story.

I don't need everyone to forgive me.

I don't need everybody to understand me.

But I do need to be able to live with myself.

And part of that means looking at my own behavior without flinching.

It also means accepting that some relationships are over, even when I wish the people involved nothing but happiness.

I can forgive someone without pretending the past didn't happen.

I can love someone without wanting them in my life.

I can acknowledge my own failures without carrying shame.

And I can respect someone else's freedom to live however they choose without surrendering my freedom to choose who belongs in my life.

I spent a long time wondering why integrity and accountability mattered so much to me.

Perhaps the answer is simpler than I thought.

**I'm going to fuck things up. I'm going to fail. I'm going to be wrong.**

I already know that.

I don't need to be perfect.

But I refuse to pretend otherwise.

And if you want to be someone significant in my life, I don't need you to be perfect either.

**Just own your shit.**

That's all.

And apparently, that's a hell of a lot to ask.
