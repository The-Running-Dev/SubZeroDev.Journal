# Discount Store Marcus Aurelius and the Flat Tire of Enlightenment

I have spent a considerable amount of my life studying Stoicism, listening to Alan Watts, and more recently exploring Zen Buddhism.

Apparently, none of these disciplines adequately prepares a man for riding an electric bicycle with a blown rear tire while spectacularly fucking drunk at five in the morning.

But here we are.

The emperor has fallen.

Twice, apparently.

Possibly on his face.

I don't remember all the details.

<!-- truncate -->

## The Emperor Has No Helmet

The weekend began innocently enough.

I was riding my e-bike through a little Bulgarian town where I've apparently become something of a local celebrity.

Not the good kind of celebrity.

More like the village idiot with disposable income and an alarming willingness to buy strangers alcohol.

I was stopped by the police.

Apparently, I was supposed to be wearing a helmet.

I didn't know that.

The officer was perfectly pleasant. Checked my identification, asked where I was from, explained the helmet situation, and sent me on my way.

No confrontation.

No drama.

Just two human beings having a perfectly reasonable interaction.

Later, I met some guys outside a store.

One of them recognized me.

Apparently, we'd met before.

I had absolutely no recollection of this.

This is becoming a recurring problem.

**The town remembers Uncle Ben. Uncle Ben does not necessarily remember the town.**

And apparently people have opinions about me.

I'm a drunk.

I'm a drug user.

I'm crazy.

I'm rich.

People are using me for my money.

Okay.

And?

What exactly am I supposed to do with this information?

I bought everyone drinks because I wanted to buy everyone drinks.

When I have money, I have money.

When I don't have money, I don't.

I could spend the rest of my life calculating whether somebody genuinely appreciates the beer I bought him.

Or I could drink my fucking beer.

Marcus Aurelius probably had similar problems with the Roman Senate.

Except he had an empire.

I have an e-bike.

Which, by the end of the night, would no longer possess a functioning rear tire.

## The Flat Tire of Enlightenment

One of the guys borrowed my bicycle to buy more booze.

He returned with a flat rear tire.

Outstanding.

At approximately five in the morning, having consumed enough alcohol to make my judgment considerably less impressive than my vocabulary, I decided to ride the thing home.

On the flat tire.

I fell.

Got up.

Apparently fell again.

At some point my face had an appointment with the pavement.

I discovered the damage to my nose the following day.

Eventually, I abandoned this magnificent demonstration of human intelligence and pushed the bicycle home.

My camper already has problems.

Now the e-bike is fucked.

My phone is somewhere else.

My hand hurts.

And the emperor is wondering what the fuck happened to his transportation department.

Would riding the bicycle drunk on a flat tire qualify as a brilliant decision?

Probably not.

It was dangerous. The consequences were real.

But here's the thing.

**I don't need to turn every event into a judgment about who I am.**

I got drunk.

I rode a bicycle.

I fell on my face.

I pushed the bicycle home.

These things happened.

I can acknowledge what happened and deal with the consequences without converting the entire evening into a fucking morality play.

Apparently, I'm supposed to experience shame.

I don't.

Or at least, that's what I thought.

We'll get to that.

## The Village Has Opinions

One of the guys told me that people talk shit about me behind my back.

Okay.

Then he offered to beat up anyone who insulted me in his presence.

Jesus fucking Christ.

My brother, relax.

Have a drink.

I don't need you to defend my honor.

What honor?

I'm a fifty-year-old man who just demonstrated that an e-bike cannot be repaired through the application of alcohol and optimism.

Some people think I'm amazing.

Others think I'm a complete fucking failure.

Both groups are entitled to their opinions.

Neither group gets to decide who I am.

And when somebody started telling me what another guy had been saying behind my back, I stopped the conversation.

He's not here.

I'm not going to talk about him behind his back.

He can say whatever he wants about me.

I don't need to participate.

This is something I genuinely believe.

**The best revenge is not to be like your enemy.**

Marcus Aurelius said it considerably more elegantly.

I said it over drinks outside a Bulgarian convenience store.

The philosophy survived the change of venue.

## The Problem That Cannot Be Solved

And then, somewhere in this magnificent weekend of philosophical sophistication and transportation disasters, I came across a short video discussing an idea attributed to Carl Jung.

Some problems aren't meant to be solved.

They're meant to be outgrown.

Holy fucking shit.

There it was.

The thing I'd been trying to explain for years.

My relationship with my mother.

Elizabeth.

A woman I've spent decades trying to understand.

A woman I admire in many ways.

A woman whose behavior has also caused me an extraordinary amount of pain.

I've watched her strength, determination, intelligence, and independence.

I've also watched her pride, controlling behavior, and inability, as I've experienced it, to acknowledge the damage she's caused.

And I've tried.

Jesus Christ, I've tried.

I even took her to therapy.

She decided the therapist couldn't help her because she was smarter than the therapist.

Well.

That certainly resolves the problem.

A year and a half of weekly therapy, and my own therapist once pointed out that roughly sixty percent of our sessions were spent talking about my mother.

Sixty fucking percent.

Apparently Elizabeth had acquired a controlling interest in my psychological real estate.

And she wasn't paying rent.

But here's what I've finally begun to understand.

**I cannot solve Elizabeth.**

I cannot make her acknowledge things she doesn't want to acknowledge.

I cannot make her understand my childhood.

I cannot make her see herself the way I've experienced her.

I cannot make her become the person I still believe she could be.

And that makes me tremendously sad.

She's approaching eighty.

I know she suffers. I've watched her break down.

I wish she could find peace.

I wish she could be happy.

I wish things had been different.

But I cannot live her life for her.

And I cannot keep sacrificing mine trying to make her understand.

I haven't completely outgrown this problem.

Her recent attempts to contact me demonstrated that quite spectacularly.

But for the first time, I have the language.

**I don't have to solve the relationship. I have to outgrow my need to solve it.**

Not quite as easy as fixing a bicycle tire.

Although my recent mechanical achievements suggest I shouldn't be trusted with that either.

## A Letter from a Thirteen-Year-Old

Then something remarkable happened.

I remembered an essay I'd written about myself as a child.

I was somewhere between twelve and fourteen.

I don't remember the assignment.

I don't remember the teacher.

I don't even remember why I wrote it.

But I still have the text.

And nearly forty years later, I read it again.

The boy described himself in the third person.

Intelligent.

Capable.

But lazy.

Lacking the willpower to accomplish what he could.

Likely to regret not fulfilling his potential.

And he included this extraordinary little observation:

> "...според родителите му, дори и според самият него."

According to his parents, and even according to himself.

Jesus fucking Christ.

I read that at fifty and felt sad for that boy.

Because I remember him.

I remember working with my father.

I remember looking after my younger brothers.

I remember dishes, chores, responsibilities, helping, working.

I was never fucking lazy.

But somehow, that was the message I'd absorbed.

Not enough.

Never enough.

And there it was, preserved in my own words, written before I even understood what I was describing.

What's astonishing is the self-awareness.

That kid was already observing himself.

Already noticing contradictions.

Already examining his motivations.

Already attempting to understand what made him who he was.

And now, nearly forty years later, I'm still doing exactly that.

Only something has changed.

**I no longer need to accept somebody else's judgment as the truth about myself.**

I don't need Elizabeth to declare that I finally did enough.

I don't need her approval.

And I don't need to pretend that the child who wrote that essay was somehow defective.

He was a kid.

A fucking kid.

Doing the best he could.

## The Mother I Never Stopped Studying

Here's another spectacular irony.

I admire Elizabeth.

I've told her so many times.

I've thanked her for things she's accomplished.

I've recognized her strengths.

And somewhere along the way, without deliberately trying, I absorbed many of those strengths myself.

But I also paid attention to the things I didn't admire.

The vengefulness.

The attempts to control people.

The unwillingness to apologize.

The pride that, in my experience, has damaged so many relationships.

And I chose differently.

Not because I wanted to defeat her.

Not because I wanted to prove I was better.

I was simply observing another human being and deciding how I wanted to live.

I learned to say thank you.

I learned to apologize.

I learned to acknowledge my mistakes.

I learned that I didn't have to retaliate every time somebody hurt me.

And here's the fucking joke.

Elizabeth once told me she never taught me to say thank you or sorry.

Oh, she taught me.

Just not quite the way she thinks.

I learned from everything I admired.

And I learned from everything I didn't.

Perhaps that's why I see so much of her in myself.

And why I wish she could recognize some of herself in me.

But again.

Not my jurisdiction.

## The Peace I Apparently Already Had

Lately, I've been falling asleep listening to a YouTube channel called Midnight Monk.

Zen.

Buddhism.

Peace.

Wholeness.

The idea that we spend our lives trying to become something, fix something, achieve something, only to discover that what we were searching for was never entirely absent.

We were already here.

Already human.

Already whole.

And I've been listening to these teachings with a remarkable sense of recognition.

Not because they're introducing me to some entirely new way of living.

But because they keep putting words to things I've sensed throughout my life.

Alan Watts did this for me.

Stoicism did this for me.

Now Zen is doing it too.

The boy who wrote that essay was already himself.

The fifty-year-old who fell off a bicycle is still himself.

I've changed enormously.

I've learned.

I've suffered.

I've made choices.

I've built things.

I've fucked things up.

And I will continue doing all of those things.

None of it requires me to become some flawless monument to human achievement.

I am a person.

That's the entire fucking qualification.

Discount Store Marcus Aurelius has been searching for enlightenment.

Apparently, the merchandise was already in the cart.

## The One Thing I Still Cannot Accept

But this is where the evening took a turn I wasn't expecting.

Because there's one part of myself I still struggle to accept.

My imagination.

I have an enormously creative mind.

I can build software, solve engineering problems, invent absurd stories, and find solutions where other people see nothing.

And that same creativity can produce some extraordinarily dark thoughts.

Cruel thoughts.

Elaborate thoughts of revenge.

Things I could imagine doing to somebody who hurt me.

I have never deliberately acted on those fantasies to cause another human being suffering.

I've had opportunities to retaliate.

I've walked away.

I've even discouraged other people from retaliating on my behalf.

But sometimes those thoughts appear.

And sometimes, once my imagination gets going, I find the scenarios entertaining.

And that fucking horrifies me.

Not because I intend to act on them.

But because I cannot believe my mind is capable of imagining that much cruelty.

I've even wondered whether carrying those thoughts somehow contributes to the conflict and suffering in my life.

I don't know.

It's a thought, not a conclusion.

And then I realized something uncomfortable.

I can accept that I get drunk.

I can accept that I fall on my face.

I can accept that I make questionable decisions.

I can accept that other people think I'm a complete fucking disaster.

But the things my own imagination can produce?

**That is the one thing that can still make me feel like a failure as a human being.**

And when I finally put that into words, I cried.

Actual tears.

Because I'd carried this privately for a long time.

And I'd never known quite what to do with it.

For a man who claims to have become comfortable with his imperfections, that's quite a fucking discovery.

The emperor has found another aisle.

Unfortunately, the merchandise is considerably darker than advertised.

## No Grand Revelation Required

I didn't put those thoughts into words because I wanted someone to fix me.

I didn't want a diagnosis.

I didn't need a lecture.

I didn't even particularly want reassurance.

I needed to get them out of my head.

That's how I process things.

I talk.

I write.

I examine.

Sometimes I laugh.

Sometimes I cry.

And sometimes I discover something about myself that I didn't know how to describe before.

The thoughts don't become actions simply because I can imagine them.

And I don't have to pretend that I like having them.

I can still choose how I behave.

That's where my principles live.

Not in proving to the world that my mind never goes somewhere dark.

But in what I actually choose to do.

I don't know whether I'll ever feel completely at peace with that part of myself.

And I don't need to invent an answer tonight.

## The Clearance-Aisle Emperor

So here we are.

One absurd weekend.

One broken bicycle.

One injured nose.

One town full of people who apparently know me better than I know them.

One mother I may never understand.

One childhood essay that's been sitting around for nearly four decades.

And one uncomfortable part of my own imagination that I'm only now learning to speak about.

Discount Store Marcus Aurelius stands beside his battered shopping cart.

The wheel is still fucked.

The emperor has no helmet.

His e-bike is out of commission.

His mother remains unresolved.

His shadow has opinions.

And humanity continues doing whatever the fuck humanity does.

He could stand there demanding that every wheel be repaired, every relationship be reconciled, every thought be purified, and every person finally understand him.

Or he could acknowledge that this is the life he's actually living.

I am fifty years old.

I haven't figured life out.

I doubt I ever will.

And somehow, the more I stop trying to force myself into somebody's definition of a good human being, the more I recognize myself.

Not a saint.

Not a monster.

Not an emperor.

Just a human being.

Sometimes compassionate.

Sometimes angry.

Sometimes brilliant.

Sometimes spectacularly fucking reckless.

And sometimes deeply unsettled by what goes on inside his own head.

I don't have to call any of that perfection.

I don't have to call it failure.

I can just acknowledge it.

The cart goes:

**Clack.**

**Clack.**

**Clack.**

And somewhere in Bulgaria, a fifty-year-old man is pushing an electric bicycle home because the rear tire is fucked and the philosophy department has suffered another operational incident.

No grand cosmic lesson.

No certificate of enlightenment.

Just life.

And Discount Store Marcus Aurelius, once again, has absolutely no idea what the fuck he's doing.

**Peace, motherfucker.**

The emperor keeps pushing the cart.
